The DeepCW decoders: what is inside, and how to run hundreds

The three neural CW models the engine runs — DeepCW v1, v2 wide and v2 narrow — layer by layer, how they differ, why their windows can neither be padded nor cached, what one channel costs, and how to spread hundreds of parallel signals over a CPU or a GPU.

The architecture is read from the pinned ONNX files themselves (sha256 as in deepcw_runtime.h: v1 ef120799…, v2 cd5ee381…, v2 narrow fc80c4ba…): operator counts, initialiser shapes, convolution attributes, quantisation nodes. The streaming side is from decode_deepcw.cpp, decode_deepcw2.cpp and deepcw_runtime.cpp; measurements from DeepCW.md. Compute figures are multiply-accumulates (MAC) counted from the layer shapes; a FLOP is two of them.

Short answers

What they are
All three are the same design: a log-magnitude spectrogram tile, a small strided 2-D conv front end, a Conformer encoder, a 42-class CTC head read greedily. None is a streaming model: every window is read whole, every frame sees every other.
v1 → v2 wide
Same encoder (6 blocks, d 192, 3.6 M parameters). v2 normalises its input by a noise level, quantises the front end to int8/uint8, swaps SiLU for ReLU there, and halves the time resolution in the first conv (30 ms output frames). About ⅓ the compute per second of audio.
v2 narrow
A different scale: 3 input bins instead of 65, 2 convs (12, 24 channels), 3 Conformer blocks at d 64, 0.2 M parameters — 1/17 of v2 wide's compute, 1/59 of v1's at their window lengths.
Quality
Equal in white noise. Under fast fading and multipath v1 reads 2–4× better than both v2 models at 0–10 dB; the stitcher is not the cause (DeepCW.md).
Hundreds on a CPU
Only the narrow model: ≈ 0.06 GMAC per channel-second, a few cores for 300 keyed channels. The wide models cost 0.1 (v2) to 0.36 (v1) of a core per channel.
Hundreds on a GPU
The way to run v1 or v2 wide at scale: 300 channels of v1 are ≈ 2.2 TFLOP/s, one mid-range GPU. It needs batches, so the channels must tick together and their windows must have equal length — the runtime's job queue already batches by length, the channels stagger their ticks on purpose.
The biggest lever
Neither device: each 1 s tick re-reads 7 s (v2) or 10 s (v1) of audio, so 85–90 % of the work is a re-read. The tick step, a narrow-first cascade and, with retraining, a streaming encoder move the bill more than any runtime tuning.
§1 · the common shape

One design at two scales

e04's models read a spectrogram tile [batch, 1, time, bins] at 12.5 Hz per bin and one frame per 15 ms (here 16 ms: the channel front end's hop, a 6.7 % stretch). The output is [batch, frames, 42] log-probabilities over , . / 0–9 ? A–Z, space and the CTC blank; a greedy CTC collapse (argmax per frame, merge repeats, drop blanks) turns it into text. No beam search, no language model.

§3 · why windows are read whole

No padding, no cache: three properties of the graph

  1. The front-end norms span time. Each GroupNorm normalises a channel group over the whole window — every time step of it. Append zeros and the mean and variance change for every real frame.
  2. The attention has no mask. Every output frame is a softmax over all frames of the window, padding included. There is no attention mask input to the graph at all.
  3. The position code is absolute. The sinusoidal table is built at run time for the window's length and added once; frame 0 is the window's start, not the stream's.

Two consequences shape everything below. A batch may only hold windows of equal length — DeepcwRuntime::worker_main takes the oldest job and every queued job of its length, where upstream zero-padded to the longest and got different readings batched than alone. And nothing computed for one tick can be reused at the next: there is no key/value cache to extend, so each tick pays for the whole window again. A model that streams would have to be trained to (§6).

§4 · what one window costs

Compute per window, and per channel-second

Counted from the layer shapes at the engine's 62.5 frames/s, for each backend's steady-state window: v1 re-reads its 10 s ring (625 frames); deepcw2 reads 4 s of left context + 2 s freeze guard + 1 s step = 7 s (438 frames). With one tick per second this is also the cost per channel-second.

Three things stand out. The linear layers dominate, so cost is close to linear in window length for v2; v1's attention is already a quarter of its bill at 10 s because it attends over twice the frames. The front end of both wide models runs mostly on zeros: 54 of the 65 bins are empty in this engine, but the convolution computes them anyway. And v2's time stride is where its ⅓ comes from — the encoder sees half the frames, attention a quarter.

Data movement is no constraint anywhere: a 7 s wide tile is 114 KB, 300 of them per second are 34 MB/s over PCIe; the narrow tile is 5 KB. What decides is arithmetic and whether the work arrives in batches.

On a CPU: the narrow model, many workers, small threads

  • Model. Only v2 narrow scales to hundreds on a desktop. The wide models are 9 (v2) to 3 (v1) channels per core.
  • Threads. Prefer parallelism across runs over parallelism inside one: workers ≈ physical cores, threads 1–2. The products of a d-64 model are too small to split well over 4 intra-op threads (the default is 4 × 2 workers), and independent runs scale nearly linearly. DeepcwRuntimeConfig::workers is clamped to 8, which a larger machine would need lifted. Leave cores for the channelizer and the engine thread.
  • Batching. The queue already batches equal-length windows (batch 32). On a CPU batching does not save arithmetic, it amortises the per-node overhead — the 4.3 → ~10 GMAC/s gap above. The staggered ticks (m_tick_phase) are right for the CPU: they spread the load over the second, and with hundreds of channels a worker still finds several windows of one length queued.
  • Quantisation. Dynamic int8 on the encoder's MatMuls (VNNI/AMX) is the usual 2–3× on a CPU, but it changes the model; it would need the bench tables re-run before it could be trusted.

On a GPU: v1 or v2 wide, in big aligned batches

  • Model. The GPU is how the better-under-fading v1 (or v2 wide) runs at scale. 2.2 TFLOP/s for 300 channels of v1 is a fraction of a current mid-range card's fp32 peak; at the 20–40 % of peak ONNX Runtime typically reaches on shapes like these it is one card, with margin. (An estimate from counts, not a measurement.)
  • Align the ticks. A GPU wants one launch for all channels, not one per channel. Today each channel ticks at its own phase ((registration × 7) mod tick), which on a GPU yields many small batches. With a common phase, every second brings one batch per window length.
  • Make lengths equal without padding. v1's windows are all 10 s once the ring is full. deepcw2's are 7 s in steady state, but shorter for a young channel and up to 12 s after the gate was shut. Snap each window's start to a length grid — 0.5 s, say — by taking a little more real left context from the 14 s ring, never by padding (§3). The stitcher's weights tolerate a window starting slightly earlier; padding they do not.
  • Cap the batch by memory. The graph spells attention out as MatMul → Softmax → MatMul, so the full score matrix is materialised: 9.4 MB per v1 window per layer, plus the softmax's output. A batch of 256 v1 windows is several GB transient; v2's 1.2 MB is easy.
  • Fixed shapes. With a length grid and a few batch sizes the shapes are a small fixed set: the CUDA execution provider stops re-searching convolution algorithms per shape, and TensorRT (fp16) or CUDA graphs become possible. fp16 against the uint8 input quantisation of v2 should be checked on cw_bench first. Bind inputs and outputs to pinned buffers (I/O binding) and keep one session, shared by the workers.
  • Replays stay on the CPU. Batched GPU results can differ in the last bits from a batch-1 CPU run; the deterministic replay path (sync) should remain CPU, batch 1.

The structural waste is the re-read: each tick advances the stream by one second and pays for seven or ten. A GPU makes that waste affordable; only a streaming model removes it.

§7 · not measured

What this page does not know

  • No GPU run of any model in this engine; the GPU figures are counts against an assumed efficiency.
  • The narrow model's batched CPU rate (the 4.3 → ~10 GMAC/s recovery is expected, not measured).
  • fp16 or int8 encoders against the bench's CER tables.
  • The cascade's trigger rule, and the stitcher's behaviour with 2 s ticks or grid-snapped window starts.

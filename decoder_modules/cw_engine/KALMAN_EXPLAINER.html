Kalman gain tracking for Morse on a Watterson channel

How a Kalman filter follows the HF fading gain of a CW signal, what changes when the keying is unknown, why that looks like a chicken-and-egg problem and is not one, and what went wrong with E. L. Bell's 1977 Bayesian Morse decoder.

Grounded in FADING_TRACKER.md, BAYESIAN_DECODING.md and ALGORITHM.md §5.2–5.4, in Bell's thesis listing, and in AG1LE's C port of it (ag1le/morse-wip, last commit 2014-08-29). The corrected decoder is the bench backend bell; BELL.md has the routine-by-routine comparison. The simulations on this page run in your browser.

Short answers

Steady carrier, coherent
Linear and Gaussian, so the Kalman filter is the exact optimum. The state is the complex gain as an AR(2) process whose poles come from the Doppler spread B.
Steady carrier, non-coherent
The envelope is Rician, not Gaussian, so a Kalman filter on it is an approximation (an EKF) and it throws away the phase continuity. With I/Q samples there is no reason to use it: the complex filter already turns into an energy detector by itself when the gain stops being predictable.
Morse, keying known
Still exact: the measurement matrix becomes the keying kt ∈ {0, 1}. In a space the filter only predicts, and its uncertainty grows.
Morse, keying unknown
A switching state-space model. The loop between "gain needs keying" and "keying needs gain" exists only if you estimate one and plug it into the other. If you condition instead (one Kalman filter per keying hypothesis, scored by its own innovation), the loop goes away. The price is a hypothesis tree that grows exponentially and has to be pruned or merged.
Does non-coherent make sense?
Only where phase is unavailable or not trustworthy (an audio envelope, an uncorrected frequency drift), or as a cheap level estimate. In this page's simulator it loses to the plain untracked energy detector below about 0 dB SNR500.
How Bell did it
Exactly the conditioning route. Each surviving path through a Morse element/speed/duration trellis carries its own scalar Kalman filter on the envelope amplitude. Up to 750 extensions per 5 ms are scored, and the best 7–25 are kept.
Why it failed in practice
The C port added a sign error to the duration model that the thesis listing does not have. It forbids any element from outlasting its nominal length at most speeds, and it runs words together. Fixed, the decoder makes 1.4–5× fewer errors than the port. What remains is Bell's own: noise constants fitted to his receiver, no idle state, and an amplitude model that grows overconfident in spaces.
§1 · the channel

What the Watterson model looks like inside one CW channel

ITU-R F.1487 describes the HF channel as a tapped delay line. Each path has a complex Gaussian gain with a Gaussian Doppler spectrum. Inside a 125 Hz CW channel the delay spread (0.5–7 ms) smears each key edge by at most two samples, and the unbiased dit already cancels that. So the paths collapse into one complex gain ht:

zt = kt · ht + nt k_t ∈ {0,1} keying, h_t complex gain, n_t ~ CN(0, σ²) E[ht h*t−τ] = P · exp(−2π² σf² τ²) Gaussian Doppler spectrum, B = 2σ_f

Two equal paths with the same spread sum to one Gaussian process with the same spectrum. That is why ITU good, moderate and poor (0.1 / 0.5 / 1 Hz) are each a single fading process to this decoder. The number that matters is how long the gain stays predictable compared with a Morse element:

The half-correlation time is τ½ = √(ln 2 / 2π²σf²) = 0.375 / B.

A state model for the gain

The Kalman filter needs a finite-order Markov model of ht. AR(1) has a Lorentzian spectrum: it trusts the gain too little at short lags and too much at long ones. AR(2) fits the flat-topped Gaussian correlation much better, and higher orders are numerically fragile (Baddour & Beaulieu 2005). The labs below use AR(2) with a double real pole p, chosen so that its correlation matches the Gaussian one at ρ = 0.5:

xt = [ht, ht−1]ᵀ, xt = F xt−1 + [wt, 0]ᵀ F = | 2p −p² | var(w) = P (1−p²)³ / (1+p²) keeps E|h|² = P | 1 0 | ρAR2(k) = pk (1 + k (1−p²)/(1+p²))

A residual carrier offset δf rotates the gain. Folding it in means multiplying the first row of F by ejω and ej2ω, with ω = 2πδf·T. If it is left out, the filter reads the rotation as fast fading. That is one reason the cw_engine AFC stays in front of the tracker.

§2 · steady carrier, coherent

A steady carrier with I/Q samples: the textbook case

With kt = 1 and complex samples, the model is linear with Gaussian noise. The Kalman filter computes the exact posterior p(ht | z1:t) = CN(μt, Vt). The recursion is the familiar one, with H = [1, 0]:

predict x⁻ = F x̂, Σ⁻ = F Σ Fᵀ + Q μ = x⁻[0], V = Σ⁻[0,0] update s = V + σ², K = Σ⁻[:,0] / s, x̂ = x⁻ + K (z − μ), Σ = Σ⁻ − K Σ⁻[0,:]

Because F, Q and H are real and the noise is circular, Σ stays real. Only the mean is complex, so this is cheap: about 100 flops per observation, or about 40 Mflop/s for 1536 channels at 250 Hz (FADING_TRACKER.md §5).

The equilibrium uncertainty V/P is the useful single number. It is small when B·T is small and the SNR is high, because many past samples then predict the next one. It rises toward 1 when the gain decorrelates faster than the noise can be averaged out. The lab below prints V/P for both coherent trackers.

§3 · steady carrier, non-coherent

Without phase: what a non-coherent Kalman filter can and cannot do

"Non-coherent" here means the tracker sees only yt = |zt| (or |zt|²), as Bell's receiver did: a band-pass filter, an envelope detector and a 200 Hz sampler. Three things go wrong for a Kalman filter on y.

  1. The amplitude is not Gauss-Markov. |h| is Rayleigh distributed and bounded at zero. A Gaussian state around a mean of √(πP)/2 is a moment fit that cannot represent a deep fade well.
  2. The observation is not Gaussian. Given A = |h|, y is Rician. At high SNR it is close to N(A, σ²/2). At low SNR it is skewed and biased upward (E[y | A = 0] = 0.886σ), which is exactly where the decoder needs the evidence. An EKF with a mean function √(A² + πσ²/4) fixes the bias but not the shape.
  3. Phase continuity is lost. With I/Q samples, the filter predicts the next complex value, phase included, so its evidence works like a matched filter against the prediction. On the envelope it can only say "about this strong", and its noise is the full envelope noise.

The coherent filter does not need a separate non-coherent mode. Its key-down log-likelihood ratio, given the predicted gain, is

llrt = ln CN(zt; μ, σ² + V) − ln CN(zt; 0, σ²)

When the gain is well known (V ≪ P) this is a matched filter against μ. When the gain is unknown (V → P, μ → 0) it becomes |z|²·(1/σ² − 1/(σ²+P)) − ln(1 + P/σ²), which is the energy detector for a Rayleigh-faded mark. A correctly specified complex tracker therefore moves between the coherent and non-coherent regimes by itself, as the Doppler spread changes.

So does a non-coherent Kalman filter make sense? As the main evidence source with I/Q samples available, no: it is strictly less informed and needs an approximate likelihood. It does make sense in three narrower roles:

  • when there is no phase, as in Bell's audio front end;
  • when the phase cannot be trusted, for example chirp or drift faster than the AFC follows;
  • as a cheap level estimate for a squelch or an SNR readout.

A principled non-coherent tracker would put a Gamma-type filter on |z|² (exponential observations) or use a particle filter, rather than a Gaussian Kalman filter on |z|.

§4 · keyed carrier, keying known

Morse with known keying: still exact

If someone hands you kt, the model stays linear-Gaussian with a time-varying measurement matrix Ht = [kt, 0]. In a space the update is skipped: the filter coasts on its prediction and V grows toward P at the rate the Doppler spread sets. In a mark it updates. Through a long word space at 1 Hz spread, the filter forgets most of the phase but keeps a useful idea of the level.

This is the pilot-symbol situation of FreeDV and the MIL-STD-188-110 modems, where key-down periods are known in advance. It is the "oracle" curve in the lab, an upper bound that no real decoder reaches.

§5 · keyed carrier, keying unknown

Unknown keying: the cyclic dependency, and why conditioning removes it

Now kt is what we want to decode. The tracker needs kt to know when to update, and the keying detector needs ht to know how strong a mark should be. On top of that the keying model (the dit length, the fist) is itself unknown at the start of an over.

Why there is no real cycle

Write the joint posterior over the continuous gain and the discrete keying history. Given a keying history k1:t, the gain is linear-Gaussian (§4), so the Kalman filter for that history gives p(ht | k1:t, z1:t) exactly. It also gives the history's marginal likelihood, one innovation at a time:

p(zt | k1:t, z1:t−1) = CN(zt; kt·μt|t−1, kt·Vt|t−1 + σ²) P(k1:t | z1:t) ∝ P(k1:t) · ∏ p(zτ | k1:τ, z1:τ−1) Morse prior × filter likelihoods

Nothing is guessed and fed back. Each hypothesis carries the gain belief that its own keying implies, and the hypotheses compete on how well they predicted the data (Magill 1965 for the filter bank, Ackerson & Fu 1970 for switching models). This is Rao-Blackwellisation: sample or enumerate the discrete part, integrate the Gaussian part analytically.

The cost is that the exact form keeps one Gaussian per keying history, 2t of them. Every practical decoder is defined by how it cuts that down:

Synchronising with a keying model that is not known yet

The tracker runs at the observation rate and never waits for a Morse decision. The keying model enters only through P(key down at t), and that comes in three increasingly sharp forms:

  1. Duty only (π ≈ 0.45): the PDA weight is σ(llrt + logit π). It needs no clock and no speed, so it works from the first sample.
  2. The lattice's forward prediction P(kt = 1 | z1:t−1). It knows that the middle of a dah is almost surely key-down and the middle of a word space almost surely not (BAYESIAN_DECODING.md C2, item 5).
  3. Joint state: the speed and duration live inside the hypothesis, as in Bell's decoder, so the timing and the gain are inferred together.

Two rules keep the feedback safe:

  • Exchange predictions, never posteriors. The tracker combines the lattice's probability before t with its own likelihood of zt. The lattice reads the LLR computed from the tracker's prediction before its update. Otherwise zt is counted twice and both sides become overconfident.
  • Start in the regime that needs nothing. Before the first mark the prior is μ = 0, V = P, so the LLR is exactly the energy detector. It needs neither a gain nor a clock. The speed can be bootstrapped from the energy's autocorrelation, and the coherent gain comes in as the filter locks, after about one coherence time of marks.

The remaining real risk is the plug-in loop for the slow parameters (σ², P, B, the dit clock), which are re-estimated from committed decisions. FADING_TRACKER.md's advice holds: make them first-class estimates with their own robust estimators, and when in doubt assume faster fading.

Lab 1: four trackers on the same fading Morse signal

12 s of "CQ CQ DE OK1RR OK1RR K" through a single Watterson path (64-sinusoid Gaussian Doppler), 5 ms observations, complex noise. All trackers are given the true σ², P = 1 and B; only the keying is hidden. "Element errors" sums each tracker's LLR over every true element and calls it a mark if the sum is positive. It isolates the quality of the evidence from the timing problem; it is not a character error rate.

Things to try. (1) Steady carrier: the envelope EKF trails the complex KF at every spread. (3) Push B to 10 Hz: mean V/P climbs toward 1 and the coherent filter's advantage shrinks to its energy limit. §6 · Bell 1977

How E. L. Bell's decoder solves it

Bell's NPS Doctor of Engineering thesis, Optimal Bayesian estimation of the state of a probabilistically mapped memory-conditional Markov process with application to manual Morse decoding (DTIC ADA046503), takes the conditioning route of §5 literally, in the envelope domain. The title describes the model:

  • Markov process: the hidden state is (letter-memory state λ, element type, speed, time in element).
  • Memory-conditional: transition probabilities depend on the previous element and on how long the current one has lasted, which makes it a semi-Markov model.
  • Probabilistically mapped: the observation depends on the state only through the key state and a random amplitude.

The description below follows the thesis's Fortran listing (pages 159–190), which AG1LE translated to C.

The pieces

  • Duration model. The probability of staying in an element is the ratio of a survival function S(b) at b = dur/nominal, a Laplace-like density centred on 1, 3, 1, 3, 7 or 14 dit units with sharpness α = 3m (dit, dah, element and character spaces), 10.5 (word space) or 14 (pause). See Lab 2.
  • Element and speed transitions. A second-order element table (Bell's Table XII: the next element given the previous mark and space). A speed change of 0, ±1 or ±2 steps at every element boundary, with probabilities 0.4 / 0.2 / 0.1. The step is 2 WPM, rising to 5 or 10 WPM after word spaces and pauses.
  • Letter memory. λ indexes the dot/dash prefix so far (memfcn, 400 states), so only valid Morse sequences survive.
  • Amplitude Kalman filter. Scalar, on the envelope, with H = key state (0 or 1).
    • During a mark: a random walk with q = 10−4 per sample.
    • During a space: the mean decays (φ < 1) with q = 0.
    • At a space-to-mark transition: a burst of variance that grows with how long the space lasted.
    The likelihood is the Gaussian innovation density.
  • Pruning. Keep the most probable extension of each of the six element types, so that a word space is never pruned out by a run of marks. Then add the next most probable ones until 99 % of the mass is saved, up to 25 paths (the port: 20). Paths with the same (λ, duration, speed) are merged.

This is the same structure as the cw_engine plan, built 45 years earlier. Bell's bank of filters on a trellis is the exact form that per-survivor processing (K6) approximates. Its weakness was cost and robustness, not the idea. It also leaves noise, signal level and frequency to fixed front-end heuristics, which FADING_TRACKER.md lists as first-class estimates (σ², P, B, δf).

§7 · what goes wrong

Why it did not work in practice: the listing against the port

AG1LE's testers reported missed word spaces, no AFC, and a noise estimator that could not track real noise (FADING_TRACKER.md §6). Each routine of the C port was compared with the Fortran listing in the thesis scan (pages 159–190). One bug comes from the port. The rest of the weaknesses are Bell's own model. Line numbers refer to ag1le/morse-wip src/ at commit 5eb011e. BELL.md has the full table.

port bug

The duration hazard turns negative when an element crosses its nominal length

In the branch b0 < 1 < b1 the listing has P1=0.5*EXP(-ALPHA*(B1-1.)). The port writes p1 = exp(-alpha*(b1-1.f)) * -.5f, so the "stay" probability comes out negative (−0.75 for a 25 WPM dit). On the 5 ms sample where an element passes its nominal length, every path that stays in it gets a negative weight. savep never keeps one, and the leaving transitions are inflated to compensate. In effect, no element may outlast its nominal duration at the path's current speed.

  • It is hidden at some speeds. When the nominal duration is an exact multiple of 5 ms (for the dit, at speeds that divide 240: 10, 12, 15, 16, 20, 24, 30, 40, 48 and 60 WPM), b0 lands exactly on 1 and the correct branch runs. The port starts its paths at 10–40 WPM, so a test at 20 WPM looks fine.
  • At other speeds a slow dah, a long character space or a hesitant word space can only be explained by a speed jump at the next boundary. Words run together (OK1RRDL5ABC, G3XYZRST): the missed word spaces.
  • Measured: on its own, the sign error raises CER 1.4–1.7× at 20 and 25 WPM.

xtrans.cxx:78; listing line 23600 (thesis p. 170)

port bug

Smaller port errors

  • Speed limits and path count. The port uses 5–80 WPM, 20 paths and starting speeds 10–40. The listing has 10–60 WPM, 25 paths and starting speeds 10–50.
  • Trellis start. The trellis starts at zero, so for the first second it reads path 0, out of bounds. ndel is read before it is set. The listing starts both with 5.
  • Character tree. Z has no dah child, so , can never be decoded. Q's dit child points at 0. A trellis step that settles several letters writes each over the last.
  • Shared state. The noise estimator, the trellis and the driver keep state in function statics, so two decoders cannot run side by side.

bmorse.h:29, spdtr.cxx, trelis.cxx, transl.cxx, noise.cxx

Bell's model

The noise constants fit only Bell's receiver

NOISE takes the smoothed minimum of the envelope over 1 s and 0.25 s (ymavg). It sets rn = 0.3·ymavg, subtracts 2.4·ymavg + 0.05 and scales by 1.1. These are Bell's empirical constants, fitted to his envelope: two resonators at about ±35 Hz, a rectifier, a 100 Hz low-pass.

Any other front end gives a different noise envelope. On a 250 Hz channel the envelope's minimum falls, NOISE reads the noise far too low, and noise decodes as text: CER above 1 at 6 dB SNR500. The port's clamp of NOISE's output to [0, 1] is a patch over the same mismatch. The bench backend emulates Bell's IF filter and keeps the clamp.

listing p. 190 (NOISE); morse-wip noise.cxx:116–120, bmorse.cxx:352

Bell's model

The amplitude filter becomes more confident while it sees nothing

In a space, MODEL sets φ = 10−2/(22.4·dit_ms) < 1 and q = 0. So the predicted variance φ²P shrinks during the very intervals that carry no information about the amplitude. The variance is restored only by an ad hoc term at the space-to-mark transition. A correct model has the variance grow toward the prior during a space (§4).

In marks, a random walk with q = 10−4 per 5 ms gives about 0.14 amplitude units of drift per second. That is far too slow for 1 Hz fading, where the gain changes completely within 0.4 s. None of these constants is tied to a measured Doppler spread.

listing p. 177–178 (MODEL)

Bell's model

Merging drops mass, and there is no idle state

  • Merging. When two saved paths share (λ, duration, speed), SAVEP keeps the first and discards the second, together with its probability. A Bayesian merge would add the probabilities and moment-match the two Kalman states.
  • No idle state. After 14 dits of pause the model must start a mark, so noise before and between overs decodes as characters. That is most of the error left in the corrected decoder.
  • Loose speed prior. Only 40 % of element boundaries keep the speed.
  • A model the listing leaves out. Bell's richer letter-tree model reads 300 more states from a file, MORSEM, that the thesis does not print. The port and the bench backend run the printed 16-state model.

listing p. 180–183 (SAVEP), p. 160–161 (INITL)

§8 · on the bench

Bell's decoder in cw_engine

The corrected decoder is the bench backend bell (src/engine/bell/, decode_bell.*). It follows the listing routine by routine and keeps Bell's names, with indices from 0. Per channel it has its own state, an emulation of Bell's IF filter, |IQ| at 200 Hz and a peak AGC. bell:port reproduces morse-wip, and bell:listing drops the two clamps, which are not in Bell's design.

On cw_engine_bench Bell's decoder needs 4–6 dB more SNR than v3 on a steady signal. Under fading the order turns: each of its paths carries its own amplitude filter, so a fade that v3 reads as a space can still be explained as a weaker mark. "q matched": the best of six values of MODEL's amplitude process noise per 5 ms (bell:q<x>). It grows as B², as the random walk of the bench's Gaussian Doppler predicts: (πBΔt)²/2 ≈ 1.2·10⁻⁴·B² with B = 2σf. Bell's fixed 10⁻⁴ is right up to about 0.5 Hz; at 3 Hz the matched value nearly halves the error rate. BELL.md has 6 and 20 dB, the other conditions and the white-noise ladder.

What is left to do

  1. Fix Bell's model one part at a time, with an A/B run for each:
    • q from an estimate of the Doppler spread instead of by hand, and variance that grows in spaces;
    • merging that adds probability mass;
    • an idle state;
    • noise from the band floor instead of NOISE's minimum tracker.
  2. Swap the observation. Replace the envelope filter with the complex AR(2) filter of this page, on the ½-dit block sums. That makes Bell's decoder the exact (pruned) form of cw_engine's K6, to measure against the soft-association tracker (K2).

Either way the result is a reference point. If a repaired Bell decoder with a complex tracker clearly beats soft association plus the lattice, per-survivor beliefs (K6) are worth building. If it does not, the plan can stop at K2–K5 with evidence behind that choice. Bell's decoder stays on the bench: up to 750 Kalman updates per 5 ms is about 150 k per second per channel, far too much for 1536 channels.
